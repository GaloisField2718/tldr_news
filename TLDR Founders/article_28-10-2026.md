HEADLINES & TRENDS

 REQUESTS FOR STARTUPS (12 MINUTE READ) [7]

 Requests for Startups is a place for Y Combinator to share the ideas
it would like to see founders tackle. Y Combinator is excited about
startups building the hardware, infrastructure, financial systems, and
specialized software needed to bring AI into the real economy. The
ideas listed in Requests for Startups represent just a fraction of
what Y Combinator funds. Founders working in other areas are still
encouraged to apply to the fund.

 NEW FOUNDER ARCHETYPES IN THE AI ERA (9 MINUTE READ) [8]

 The solo founder is one of the archetypes we'll likely soon see a lot
more of. AI enables solo founders to do 100x more, allowing them to
ship a v1 when it wasn't possible before. People who can create but
can't code can now build. Experts can now create their own software to
solve niche problems. Kids and teenagers, who already build products
on platforms like Minecraft and Roblox, can now build way more with
AI. There will likely be a high failure rate, but some will inevitably
work out.

STRATEGIES & TACTICS

 THE EARLY ACCESS PLAYBOOK V2.0 (69 MINUTE READ) [9]

 AI made it easy to ship every week and just as easy to skip asking
customers whether they need what you shipped. This playbook (from a
founder who lost about 18 months to two wrong bets at KISSmetrics)
walks through how to run early access so you learn from customers as
fast as you ship. The core habit is pretty simple, which is to ask
people about the last time they did the job and then watch them use
your product. People coming back to use it again tells you far more
than any interview, and each of the six plays comes with a short
template to fill in.

 IF INTELLIGENCE IS GETTING CHEAP, WHAT REMAINS EXPENSIVE? (8 MINUTE
READ) [10]

 Buying established service businesses gives AI builders access to
what's hardest to replicate: customer trust, distribution, and
employees' hard-earned expertise. Ownership also lets them redesign
entire workflows and capture the resulting profit gains, which would
otherwise largely accrue to their customers. The challenge is turning
hours saved into higher revenue and better service, rather than
treating adoption alone as success.

 HOW TO PITCH REALLY DAMN WELL (5 MINUTE READ) [11]

 Investors walk into early meetings a bit skeptical, mostly looking
for a reason to pass on you. A former YC partner explains how to build
your pitch around a few claims that make passing feel a little
riskier. Investors still don't know how to judge AI-era companies (and
they've all watched a huge one slip away), so one tactic is to pick
three or four claims where believing any one of them would make an
investor nervous to say no. For example, you can test those claims in
investor meetings six to twelve months before you raise and keep the
ones people still repeat back to you a quarter later.

MISCELLANEOUS

 GOOD INTERFACES MAKE GOOD NEIGHBORS (3 MINUTE READ) [16]

 Good interfaces allow independent systems to work together without
knowing everything about one another. Collaboration problems between
teams are often interface problems rather than relationship problems.
A good interface makes it obvious who owns what, what they are working
on, and how new requests are evaluated. When those things aren't
clear, even well-intentioned people end up wasting energy on
disagreements and reverse-engineering decisions.

 HOW ORAN AKRON REBUILT MONDAY'S $1B GTM ENGINE WITH AGENTS (13 MINUTE
READ) [17]

 Putting an AI agent on a sales team is mostly a people problem, since
reps tend to assume it's coming for their jobs. monday.com's former VP
of AI GTM walks through how they got a voice agent making the first
call on every new lead without a fight. The trick was to start small,
so the agent only got more leads once it was doing as well as the reps
on the ones it already had. New leads used to wait about 25 hours for
a call back, and now it's under two minutes (the team still spends 20
minutes every morning going over call scores).
