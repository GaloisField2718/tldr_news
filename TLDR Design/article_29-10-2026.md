 WE'RE ANNOUNCING GENIE 4, OUR NEW WORLD MODEL FOR REAL-TIME
INTERACTIVE SIMULATION (1 MINUTE READ) [4]

 Google's Genie 4, a new world model for real-time interactive
simulation, brings more realistic visuals and native audio to
generated worlds. Natural-language prompts steer scenes live, from
moving the camera to changing the weather or adding characters.
Rollout starts with a limited group of trusted testers, with Google AI
Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and early-access Gemini API
partners coming soon.

 APPLE LAUNCHES ITS NEW TOUCHSCREEN MACBOOK PRO (2 MINUTE READ) [5]

 Apple's first touchscreen MacBook Pro comes in 14- and 16-inch sizes,
pairing a PROLED XDR display with a Dynamic Island-style cutout and a
lighter body. A redesigned hinge keeps the screen steady during touch
input, while M5 Pro and M5 Max chips power both models. Prices start
at $2,899 for the 14-inch model and $3,399 for the 16-inch model, with
preorders opening October 30 and availability beginning November 6.

 GOOGLE PHOTOS ROLLS OUT A CUSTOMIZABLE REDESIGN OF YOUR
‘COLLECTIONS' TAB (3 MINUTE READ) [6]

 Google Photos on Android is getting a customizable Collections tab
that lets users pin frequently opened collections, hide sections they
rarely use, and rearrange the layout. Favorites, Screenshots, and
Trash are pinned by default, while albums, people and pets, places,
and other categories get carousels that can be hidden or reordered.
The update arrives with Google Photos version 7.97 through a
server-side switch, with no broader release date announced.

 DESIGNING FOR PEOPLE WITH ADHD (9 MINUTE READ) [7]

 ADHD affects about 5% of people and stems from differences in how the
brain develops focus, planning, and self-regulation. It is not a
matter of willpower. Helpful patterns include reminders for unfinished
drafts, chunked long forms, gradual disclosure of options, and visible
progress, while autoplay and infinite scroll are best avoided. ADHD
often overlaps with autism, dyslexia, and anxiety, so designing for it
tends to help several groups at once.

 A SELECT SHOULD TELL YOU SOMETHING (6 MINUTE READ) [8]

 Customizable HTML selects can now include previews, secondary
information, warnings, and other context that helps people recognize
the right option instead of deciphering text labels. Practical
examples include showing fonts in their actual typefaces, displaying
current local times instead of UTC offsets, aligning delivery prices
for comparison, and distinguishing people with identical names by job
title and department. The technique progressively enhances the native
`select`, so unsupported browsers can retain familiar controls while
richer desktop experiences provide more useful information without
replacing built-in semantics and keyboard behavior.

 ONE SIZE DOESN'T FIT ALL: RETHINKING “GOOD DESIGN” ACROSS
CULTURES (9 MINUTE READ) [9]

 Ideas such as clarity, simplicity, restraint, and functionality are
often treated as universal measures of good design even though they
emerged from particular Western histories and cultural contexts.
Design traditions excluded from the dominant canon can use decoration,
complexity, symbolism, and craft in ways that are deeply functional
within their cultures, challenging assumptions that minimalism is
inherently more sophisticated or understandable. Evaluating design
more broadly requires asking who established prevailing standards of
taste, whose histories were documented and taught, and whether a
design successfully serves its specific cultural purpose rather than
conforming to a supposedly universal aesthetic.

 THE SOFTWARE FACTORY FORGOT ABOUT DESIGN (4 MINUTE READ) [13]

 A four-station workflow of Linear, Paper, a coding agent, and review
brings design into the software factory, where issues otherwise turn
straight into pull requests. Linear holds the intent, Paper hosts
agent-drafted HTML and CSS designs made before implementation, and the
coding agent builds from both issue and design. Review checks the
build against intent, but agents still lack the history behind
decisions, such as past designs, analytics, and design-system
reasoning.

 THE NEW YORK TIMES TURNS 175 WITH AN IDENTITY, EVENTS AND A CAMPAIGN
(6 MINUTE READ) [14]

 The New York Times is marking its 175th anniversary with a visual
identity, campaign, and program of events that use the publication's
history as material rather than simply commemorating its age. Familiar
elements from its editorial and typographic heritage are reframed
across contemporary applications, connecting decades of journalism
with the publication's current cultural role. The system demonstrates
how an established institution can celebrate recognizable brand equity
while giving historical assets new relevance instead of replacing
them.

 HOW MOTHER DESIGN TURNED A SÉANCE INTO A NEW IDENTITY FOR PURPLE
GARDEN (6 MINUTE READ) [15]

 Mother Design began Purple Garden's rebrand with a séance, chakra
reading, and automatic drawing workshop, using the resulting forms and
feelings to build an identity around spirituality as “A Living
Practice.” A whimsical wordmark, crystal-like guidance glyph, warm
purple palette, tactile imagery, living typography inspired by cymatic
patterns, and expressive motion create an immersive system without
relying on familiar psychic-industry clichés. The process moved
between physical experimentation, traditional design software, and AI
tools, deliberately introducing unpredictability into both the
creative process and the finished identity.
